## LinkedIn Post

Retail display packaging usually breaks after the first store setup, not after the first quote.

That happens when the buyer requests a display box without defining how the unit will ship, how it will be stocked, and what the retailer expects on the shelf.

For counter and shelf programs, a stronger quote request usually answers:

1. What shelf footprint, counter depth, or retail-facing dimensions does the display need to hit?
2. How many units should each display hold, and how fast will store teams need to replenish it?
3. Is the format a countertop tray, a shelf-ready case with a tear-away panel, a PDQ, or a compact floor display?
4. What product weight and total loaded weight should the structure support?
5. Which artwork panels, barcode zones, and shopper-facing claims need to remain visible after setup?
6. Will the display ship pre-loaded, flat-packed, or inside a master carton for store assembly?
7. What quantity, launch date, and replenishment cadence should the quote assume?

That matters because a low first quote does not help if the display sags under load, wastes shelf space, or creates a slow in-store restock process.

The latest Apex guide walks through how buyers can define retail display boxes around store footprint, board strength, visibility, replenishment, and delivery planning before comparing price.

Retail display packaging works better when the quote starts with the real shelf plan, not just the carton shape.

Before asking for pricing, it helps to confirm:

- shelf footprint, counter depth, and display height
- unit count per display and restock frequency
- tray, shelf-ready case, PDQ, or compact floor-display format
- loaded weight and route to store
- artwork panels, barcode zones, and shopper-facing claims
- launch date, replenishment packs, and delivery requirements

That gives the packaging team enough detail to recommend the right board strength, tear-away setup, shipper format, and approval path before the proof stage turns into rework.
